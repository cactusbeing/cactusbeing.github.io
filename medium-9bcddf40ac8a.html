How many of the “toxic chicken soup” sayings you’ve heard since childhood have left a lasting impact on you?

發佈者：仙人掌星人 · 2025-04-14 18:14（台灣時間）

Why do so many so-called rational quotes popular in Asia make you feel increasingly guilty over time?

“You’re just too sensitive.”
 “You need to learn to see things from a different perspective.”
 “You’re the one who allowed the other party to cross the line.”

Sound familiar?

In many Asian cultures, we’re taught to be sensible, considerate, tolerant, self-reflective — even grateful for pain. These sayings, often packaged as wisdom or tools for self-growth, appear to encourage maturity.
 But in reality, they quietly teach us to suppress our emotions, stay silent, and blame ourselves. We end up alone — left only with self-doubt and guilt.

This article presents a collection of commonly heard “toxic growth talk” in Asia and breaks down why these seemingly reasonable phrases actually leave us feeling more wronged and isolated as we grow up.

How many of these phrases have hit home for you? Let’s detox them — together.

❶ “You have to learn to think from a different perspective. The other person may have their own struggles.”

🧠 Sounds like: Empathize with others and try to understand where they’re coming from.
 ☠️ In reality: You’re being asked to suppress your hurt and rationalize the behavior of someone who hurt you.

🔍 Solution:
 Empathy should go both ways. When someone is hurt, the priority should be to validate their emotions — not to ask them to sympathize with the one who caused the harm.
 “Understanding” does not mean “forgiving” or “letting go.”
 You have every right to say no to harmful behavior, and your emotional response is valid.

❷ “You’re too sensitive. You need to toughen up.”

🧠 Sounds like: Build mental resilience and emotional independence.
 ☠️ In reality: Your feelings are being belittled, and you’re being told to stay quiet and not fight back.

🔍 Solution:
 Sensitivity is not a flaw, and emotions are not a weakness.
 True resilience is not about being emotionless, but about being able to recognize, process, and express emotions.
 If “strength” means denying your pain, it’s not growth — it’s repression.

❸ “You’re unhappy because you care too much about what others think.”

🧠 Sounds like: Live your own life and stay present.
 ☠️ In reality: Dismissing the fact that social judgment can genuinely harm people — especially those in vulnerable positions.

🔍 Solution:
 You can’t always “stop caring.” Social judgment is real, and its consequences are real.
 Rather than blame individuals for caring, let’s ask:
 Why is it so easy for society to judge and label people in the first place?

❹ “Maybe you should reflect on whether you’ve done something wrong first.”

🧠 Sounds like: Practice self-awareness and take responsibility.
 ☠️ In reality: Blames the victim, making them think they deserved the mistreatment.

🔍 Solution:
 Self-reflection is important, but it should not become a habit of self-blame.
 When someone disrespects you, it’s not because you weren’t “good enough.” It’s because they chose to be disrespectful.
 You can grow from experience, but don’t take responsibility for someone else’s actions.

❺ “Don’t blame your family for your problems. You’re an adult now.”

🧠 Sounds like: Be mature, be responsible.
 ☠️ In reality: Ignores the deep, long-term effects of childhood trauma and structural issues. Cuts off empathy for those still healing.

🔍 Solution:
 Acknowledging the harm done by your upbringing is not “blaming.” It’s understanding.
 You can take charge of your life and still hold space to grieve, repair, and seek support for what you went through.
 That’s not weakness — that’s healing.

❻ “You’re miserable because you’re not grateful enough.”

🧠 Sounds like: Practice gratitude and attract positivity.
 ☠️ In reality: Silences real pain with toxic positivity.

🔍 Solution:
 Gratitude is not a painkiller.
 Telling someone who’s struggling with trauma, anxiety, or survival stress to “just be grateful” is like telling someone who’s bleeding, “Hey, at least your other hand’s fine.”
 True healing allows for pain. It doesn’t shame you for feeling it.

❼ “It’s your fault for letting people push your boundaries.”

🧠 Sounds like: Learn to set boundaries and protect yourself.
 ☠️ In reality: Blames victims for being hurt. Shifts all the responsibility onto them.

🔍 Solution:
 What this really says is: “You were violated because you didn’t defend yourself.”
 That’s not empowerment — that’s emotional blackmail.

Boundary-setting is a two-way street. It only works in a culture that listens and respects differences.
 But let’s be real:

  • Many of us were never taught to say “no.”
  • Many live under power imbalances (e.g., boss vs. employee, men vs. women, elders vs. juniors).
  • Many are just beginning to understand what boundaries even are.

We can — and should — encourage people to protect themselves.
 But first, we must acknowledge that harm was done, and stop placing all the blame on those who didn’t know how to stop it.

Because this kind of language?
 It sounds like it’s empowering you…
 But what it’s really saying is: “You deserved it.”

✊ Final Thoughts:
 Real self-growth isn’t about becoming more silent, more tolerant, or more repressed.
 It’s about learning where your boundaries are, understanding your emotional truth, and building a life where you are safe to feel, speak, and be heard.
